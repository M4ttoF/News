## CVs and cover letters are obsolete. What’s a hiring manager to do 🤷🏽‍♂️?

The entire recruitment process is tottering under the weight of AI: latest figures from Greenhouse, a hiring platform, suggest that 87 per cent of UK recruiters are seeing AI use in at least half of applications they receive. Overwhelmed staff receive high volumes of low-quality applications for every job, with half of all recruiters saying this is their biggest problem. As Greenhouse’s chief people officer, [Sharawn Tipton](https://archive.ph/o/CobS3/https://www.linkedin.com/in/sharawnconnors/), says: “When every application is polished, polish tells you nothing.” She notes that fewer than 7 per cent of applicants reach the interview stage.

[Judd Kessler](https://archive.ph/o/CobS3/https://www.linkedin.com/in/juddkessler/), a professor of business economics and public policy at the Wharton School at the University of Pennsylvania, has [researched the decline of cover letters](https://archive.ph/o/CobS3/https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/podcast/this-week-in-business/the-decline-of-the-cover-letter-in-the-ai-era/) in the age of AI. Economists worry about “cheap talk”, Judd says — which is what cover letters have become, meaning that “people send messages that don’t have any information content”.

The answer to this “cheap talk”? Increasingly, recruiters are bypassing perfect identikit applications and relying on personal connections and recommendations from their network. Judd and his colleagues looked at how letters of recommendation from a former boss or teacher can help boost candidates’ chances: “The signalling value for the letter of recommendation comes from the fact that the person was willing to send it,” Judd says — even if AI has helped draft that letter. “Even before AI, a lot of letter writers were getting the person they were writing for to do the first draft.” There’s a time cost for the person doing the recommending to send the letter ⏰— and a higher cost to their reputation if the candidate fails in the job. When professional credibility is on the line, it creates a high-value signal.

To test how letters of recommendation help with careers, the Wharton team did some research with participants in New York City’s [summer youth employment programme](https://archive.ph/o/CobS3/https://www.nyc.gov/site/dycd/services/jobs-internships/summer-youth-employment-program-syep.page). This huge scheme gives NY residents aged 14-24 a chance to explore career paths and get paid work experience (is anyone copying this in the UK?).

After completing the programme, some young workers were randomly assigned to receive letters of recommendation from supervisors. The researchers followed them for several years, and saw significant benefits for the people who used letters to support job applications. “We found they are more likely to get jobs faster,” Judd says, adding that they are more likely to find work in industries they are interested in.

[Victoria McLean](https://archive.ph/o/CobS3/https://www.linkedin.com/in/mcleanvictoria/), chief executive of career and recruitment consultancies City CV and Hanover Talent Solutions, says letters of recommendation “never went away” but sees a wider trend in “the return of trusted human recommendations”. She has run recruitment teams “that have invested significant time and money in a search, only for a trusted referral to emerge and completely change the direction of the hiring process”.

Referrals are not a new phenomenon: many employers (including the FT) pay a bonus to staff who refer someone who is then recruited. And long-term relationships with headhunters have long been important for senior professionals; nurturing those contacts is even more important now, Victoria says: “When an employer has worked successfully with a recruiter over many years, that recruiter’s recommendation carries real weight. A good recruiter is staking their own reputation on every shortlist they present.”

Any expansion of recommendation and referral-based hiring will probably reduce workforce diversity. As Sharawn at Greenhouse says: “The instinct to trust someone your team vouches for is understandable, but leaning on personal networks reinforces existing inequities, and referrals tend to give you more of the same.” She suggests that hiring companies counter this by letting candidates demonstrate what they can do earlier in the recruitment process.

* **In a nutshell:** Recommendations and referrals are helping candidates bypass AI-driven recruitment. It’s human nature to favour people known to us, or who are like us in some aspect — [homophily](https://archive.ph/o/CobS3/https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-66415-2); but this trend will probably make it harder for recruiters to keep processes fair and inclusive.

* **Want more?** Harvard Business School’s report on “[Hidden Workers, Untapped Talent](https://archive.ph/o/CobS3/https://www.hbs.edu/managing-the-future-of-work/research/hidden-workers-untapped-talent)” outlines how to find and recruit people “hidden” and excluded by automated hiring processes.

## A word from the Working It community ✍🏻

More news about CEOs’ [novel staff communication strategies](https://archive.ph/o/CobS3/https://www.ft.com/content/cf464ffa-ef04-4f50-8210-4ed06b85932c), this week featuring [Spencer Rascoff](https://archive.ph/o/CobS3/https://www.linkedin.com/in/spencerrascoff/), chief executive of dating company Match Group, which also owns Hinge and Tinder. He’s created an internal channel for staff called “DM Me If…”. (I like this on-brand tip from a dating app CEO 😍). Spencer explains how it works:

> “Any of our employees can message me directly — with an idea, a question, a complaint, anything. No hierarchy, no filters. I read every single message. If someone includes their name, I follow up with them directly; if they stay anonymous, I answer publicly so the whole company sees it. And it’s had a real impact.”

One example he cites is a message from a Gen Z employee, *“*who asked if I’d actually use our Gen Z employee group as a sounding board — I now meet with them monthly, and their unfiltered feedback has changed how I think about our products*”.*

Spencer also opted to have his mid-year performance review done live at an all-hands meeting, in front of all employees 😳. I would call this “extreme CEO-ing”: *“*Was it a bit uncomfortable? Sure. But people won’t speak up unless they see honesty flowing in both directions — and I think trust is built in the moments that feel slightly uncomfortable*.”*
