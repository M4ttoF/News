## Wall Street heavyweights line up $500bn for Nvidia

Last week, DD delved into the [“alchemy” being conjured](https://www.ft.com/content/d1f10ede-f73f-4bdf-9aa6-b6ac028f63ce?syn-25a6b1a6=1) by the likes of **Apollo** and **Blackstone** to help tech giants fund the build-out of AI, highlighting the FT’s deep dive into a complex but novel $35bn financing. We predicted such deals “may grow larger yet”.

On Monday, the FT scooped [**Nvidia**’s plan to raise more than $500bn](https://www.ft.com/content/98a8fd17-15b6-4f67-9cb4-825722b11348?syn-25a6b1a6=1) from the two private capital giants, and **BlackRock**, **Brookfield**, **Goldman Sachs** and **KKR**, to plough into AI.

Nvidia has formed a financing partnership with the six groups to raise over half a trillion dollars in “compute financing platforms” that will fund spending across its “ecosystem, including leading frontier AI labs, enterprises and AI clouds”.

The effort is arguably the most ambitious financing plan to ever hit Wall Street and it underscores how tech companies are turning to private capital giants to unearth the cash and efficiently fund the next leg of the AI build-out.

Under the deal, which is still subject to final agreement, the groups will create dedicated pools of capital to finance Nvidia’s AI ambitions “at attractive rates for Nvidia customers”.

While Nvidia’s specific plans are scant, they reflect the fact that public markets alone cannot handle spending burdens of hyperscalers, AI algorithms and so-called neoclouds that may reach over $8tn in the coming years.

Increasingly, the likes of Apollo and Blackstone are holding themselves — and their clients ranging from insurance companies, pensions and everyday savers — as willing lenders to this build-out.

Private capital has been toying with various structures to spend tens or hundreds of billions of dollars without sinking any one part of the AI ecosystem. To achieve this, everyone must do their share.

Already, groups such as Nvidia are contributing assets, while hyperscalers such as **Meta** are offering sturdy contracts to match spending plans.

In an **X** post, Nvidia chief **Jensen Huang** said the company may provide a “residual-value support mechanism” for up to 25 per cent of the value of projects its partners decide to finance.

These types of guarantees are what’s needed to then unlock the growing reservoirs of cash sitting inside Apollo, Blackstone, KKR and others that just want to earn a return that’s a bit larger than a typical investment-grade rated bond.

The Nvidia deal also reflects a now obvious but massive change on Wall Street. Groups such as Apollo, Blackstone and KKR, once known as mercenary leveraged buyout shops, have put themselves forward as regular-way financiers to AI, energy and infrastructure that they believe could be their greatest opportunity ever.

In other words, they are just capital allocators putting it on the line every day.

## Ray-Ban-wearing heirs fight over an Italian fortune

When tycoon **Leonardo Del Vecchio** died in 2022, he left to his heirs a large slice of corporate Italy and the seeds of a years-long power struggle.

The Italian billionaire made a rags-to-riches rise from a Milan orphanage to found the eyewear company **Luxottica**.

He grew it to become the world’s largest eyewear group, acquiring brands such as **Ray-Ban** and merging with France’s **Essilor** in 2018 to form **EssilorLuxottica**.

The [source of strife](https://www.ft.com/content/a38ffc0f-912d-416f-ac6e-75414072a370?syn-25a6b1a6=1) today is **Delfin**, the €55bn holding company at the heart of the Del Vecchio family fortune, the FT’s Silvia Sciorilli Borrelli reports.

Under Del Vecchio, Delfin became one of Europe’s most influential family investment vehicles. It used its multibillion-euro stakes in EssilorLuxottica, Italy’s biggest insurer **Generali** and lenders **Mediobanca** and **UniCredit** to shape corporate strategy across Italy’s financial and industrial sectors.

But since Del Vecchio’s death, Delfin has been paralysed. The deadlock stems from safeguards created by the Luxottica founder to protect his business empire, including bylaws that require the support of at least five of its eight shareholders for key decisions.

The shareholders are Del Vecchio’s six children, as well as his widow and her son by a previous marriage, who each hold an equal stake in Delfin. They’ve argued over everything from dividends and executive pay to the future of the group’s portfolio.

In June, the depth of the paralysis became clear when **Leonardo Maria Del Vecchio** sought the board’s backing for a proposal to buy out two of his siblings’ stakes. Leonardo Maria failed to win over the board, all but ending his attempt to become the group’s dominant shareholder.

The family’s divisions have reverberated across corporate Italy as the group struggles to take strategic decisions, limiting its ability to deploy capital, reshape its portfolio or play the activist role that helped set Italy’s financial agenda for years.

And there’s no end in sight to the gridlock: At Delfin’s annual meeting this summer, shareholders failed to agree on higher dividends or on allowing family members to transfer their stakes into personal holding vehicles.

## How the UAE rehabilitated its image in Washington

Twenty years ago, in the aftermath of the 9/11 attacks, the United Arab Emirates ran into a problem doing business in the US: the Gulf state had become politically toxic.

When the Dubai-owned logistics company **DP World** attempted to buy up six major American seaports in 2006, it spurred such an overwhelming backlash in Washington that DP World abandoned the deal.

In response, UAE officials made a concerted — and expensive — effort to win over Washington. Their success is obvious as US politicians and military commanders today view the UAE as one of America’s most capable allies in the Middle East.

Greasing the wheels of the diplomatic relationship is the [$1.7tn that Abu Dhabi manages](https://www.ft.com/content/ec5f7351-7aec-4fe1-80dc-b02c62045109?syn-25a6b1a6=1) in sovereign wealth funds and state-backed businesses, the FT reports in a Big Read. It has invested heavily in America, and in particular in the tech industry.

**MGX**, a tech company chaired by the UAE’s national security adviser and brother to the president, **Sheikh Tahnoon bin Zayed al-Nahyan**, has launched expansive partnerships with groups such as **OpenAI** and **xAI**.

Last September, MGX took a 15 per cent stake in **TikTok** as part of a US-led consortium that struck a deal with Washington.

Other Abu Dhabi entities, including **G42**, another company controlled by Sheikh Tahnoon, have also made significant investments in US tech.

Democrats have scrutinised the Trump administration’s overtures to the UAE, including a recent decision to grant the Emiratis access to some of the US’s most advanced computer chips. Critics of the move have seized on reports that aides of Sheikh Tahnoon purchased a 49 per cent stake in the Trump family’s crypto business, **World Liberty Financial**, shortly before the president’s inauguration last year.

The next US administration may well take a harder line with the UAE. But now that the Gulf state’s money flows through Wall Street and Silicon Valley, the ties between the two countries will be more difficult for future leaders to unwind.
