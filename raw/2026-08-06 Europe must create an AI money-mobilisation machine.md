# Europe must create an AI money-mobilisation machine

It’s a sadly familiar tale. Europe invents new technologies; then the US and China best exploit them. That’s been the story with the World Wide Web, MP3 audio compression, lithium-ion batteries and high-efficiency solar cells. Will AI be any different?

For the moment, that seems extraordinarily unlikely. Although several European AI researchers, including Jürgen Schmidhuber, Geoffrey Hinton and Demis Hassabis, helped pioneer modern AI systems, it has been US and Chinese companies that have rushed to develop and deploy them.

The big US tech companies have launched the most powerful proprietary frontier AI models, such as Claude, ChatGPT and Gemini. Chinese developers have built smaller and more adaptable open models, including DeepSeek, Qwen and Kimi. With their combined control over chip design and data centres, the two tech superpowers now dominate the global AI stack.

The consequences for Europe, if that does not change, have been spelled out in a disturbing recent essay titled “Europe 2031: What Getting AI Wrong Means for Us”. The essay’s authors, including several AI researchers and investors, highlight the dangers of falling further behind in this transformational technology. Unless Europe changes trajectory, they warn, it will “lose the ability to shape its own future”. Not only will Europe be politically and economically sidelined, it will be unable to defend its values or sustain its welfare states.

There are, though, several good things going right in Europe that could change the game over the next five years. Europe is bursting with talented researchers and entrepreneurs, who have helped create a dynamic early-stage start-up ecosystem. Both the EU and the UK are now focused on building sovereign capabilities, particularly in AI-enabled defence tech, and have launched publicly backed scale-up funds. And Europe’s strong industrial base gives it a shot at competing in the emerging fields of robotics and physical AI that fuse hardware and software.

The Finnish entrepreneur Peter Sarlin, who built and sold Silo AI for $665mn, says Europe can flourish by applying AI across every sector. “That’s where you own the customer relationship and the data flywheel and you’re in the best position to build a long-term competitive edge,” he tells me.

To that end, Sarlin’s family office PostScriptum, boosted by his proceeds of the Silo AI sale, has been investing in a new generation of AI-enabled start-ups such as Legora, Lovable, CuspAI, NestAI and Advanced Machine Intelligence.

Moreover, Sarlin argues, Europe can position itself for the next leap forward in computing by double-clicking on its expertise in quantum. Just as graphics processing units (GPUs) powered the generative-AI boom, so quantum processing units (QPUs) might accelerate the next transition in computing. Sarlin’s latest start-up QuTwo is among those developing QPUs.

Europe has been attacked for its onerous regulations that gum up innovation. But one of the biggest constraints on deploying AI is public distrust of the technology. Perversely, that may create an opportunity for Europe if it can now strike the right balance.

Meredith Whittaker, president of the Signal Foundation, says the AI models run by Big Tech companies raise concerns about privacy, security, sovereignty, autonomy and agency. Establishing a democratically accountable governance regime for AI could help Europe leapfrog into the future. Whittaker has joined PostScriptum as an adviser to help build trustworthy AI.

Though welcome, such private-sector initiatives need to be magnified to make a real difference. The Europe 2031 authors argue that the response is 10 to 100 times too small and demands the “most ambitious political agenda in the history of postwar Europe”.

Much criticism has rightly been levelled at political leaders for not doing more to act on Mario Draghi’s 2024 report on European competitiveness. Only 60 of his 383 recommendations have been fully implemented, according to the European Policy Innovation Council’s July report.

But it is not just the public sector that is failing to rise to the challenge. Corporate leaders, capital market bosses and fund managers must also tackle the €800bn annual investment shortfall identified by Draghi.

The genius of American and Chinese capitalism is their ability to direct torrents of capital at fast-evolving technologies. That can lead to wasteful over-investment and speculative bubbles. But it does not diminish the urgency for European capitalists to build their own AI money-mobilisation machine.
