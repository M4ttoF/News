# Business development companies are paying more to borrow. But why?

BDCs account for about a quarter of the total middle-market private credit market assets that [normal people call private credit](https://archive.is/o/WOGCt/https://www.ft.com/content/dcc7c956-858c-4fed-9820-f87f73f7bf8d). According to Bloomberg data, around half of the median BDC’s total assets are debt-financed, though this drops to 43 per cent on an asset-weighted basis — reflecting the slightly more conservative capital structures of many larger BDCs.

A [new note](https://archive.is/o/WOGCt/https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/the-price-of-bank-funding-behind-private-credit-evidence-from-business-development-companies-20260807.html) from [Sharjil Haque](https://archive.is/o/WOGCt/https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/sharjil-m-haque.htm) and [Jessie Wang](https://archive.is/o/WOGCt/https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/jessie-wang.htm), two Federal Reserve economists, takes a look at this BDC debt — at least that portion owed to banks rather than bond investors. The research is interesting because it comes not from the usual financial stability angle, but from a monetary policy transmission perspective. After all, with more and more borrowing from US firms occurring through private credit, it’s important for the Fed to have a sense as to what happens to the provision of credit through non-bank channels when they hike or cut. And tl;dr: they reckon the structure of lending arrangements means BDCs get rinsed for their borrowing during hiking cycles.

Accessing secret-squirrel FR Y-14Q Schedule H.1 loan-level data that is part of banks’ regulatory reporting, the economists were able to pull together the following chart:

![](https://dffna3nxhfv5z9.archive.is/WOGCt/306cafb0932099d7f8fa7e92c192db3516f0b0d4.avif)

Bank loan rates to BDCs rose more sharply during tightening © Federal Reserve, based on FR Y-14Q Schedule H.1 data

This shows that outside of tightenings, BDC and non-BDC borrowers — controlling for internal bank credit assessments — paid broadly similar average rates on loans. But as the Fed raised rates, BDC loan rates balloon.

We know what you’re thinking: when interest rates rise, the shakiness of leveraged middle-market firms that borrow from private credit will increase. So it makes sense for banks to add a bit more risk premium on their BDC interest charges.

But, argue the researchers, this doesn’t fit the facts. If banks’ forward-looking credit assessments had deteriorated, this would be reflected in the, erm, internal credit assessments (to which they have access, and for which they normalise). Moreover, they say that banks “reported lower loss-given-default estimates for BDC loans”, and that the combination of “higher rates, senior claims, collateral protection, and lower loss-given-default estimates — suggests that the BDC premium is unlikely to reflect only compensation for credit risk”.

Eyeballing the chart, we’d add that it’s not like spreads on underlying private credit loans really skied during the last couple of hiking cycles either, even if [underlying quality deteriorated](https://archive.is/o/WOGCt/https://www.ft.com/content/d49d4ab5-dfcc-4f46-9ae7-2df231c1b421):

So what’s going on? Partly, compensation for committing liquidity and balance sheet capacity — as much of bank credit comes in the form of credit lines that could be tapped when market conditions deteriorate rather than as ordinary loans. But mostly, they think, banks are exercising their enormous bargaining power.

The market for bank funding to BDCs is unusually concentrated. The top three banks account for around half the market, and the top ten more than 80 per cent. Furthermore, for whatever reason, BDCs pretty much never change their bankers:

> Much of the expansion in bank credit to BDCs occurs through renegotiation of existing credit lines rather than new loan origination. In these renegotiations, incumbent banks are naturally positioned to bargain over both credit limits and loan pricing. When BDC demand for bank funding rises during monetary tightening, relationship frictions may limit effective competition and strengthen incumbent banks’ bargaining position.

This is at least consistent with the idea that banks are squeezing BDCs in the upstream funding market, where competition is largely absent. Which in turn means (for the Fed) that the price of bank funding matters for private credit, and that monetary policy will transmit through it. 🥳

Interesting stuff.

But while Alphaville are absolutely not contesting that finding, it does seem . . . a bit odd.

While banks are a big source of BDC finance, they’re not the *only* source. Bond markets lend them bucketloads of cash. We grabbed the ICE BofA triple-B corporate bond index to see quite how high spreads were on individual BDC bonds. And the answer is high: higher than [SpaceX and Oracle](https://archive.is/o/WOGCt/https://www.ft.com/content/3a023b95-66c3-41e1-b0ce-df752a499541), and higher than average double-B spreads across maturities.

And this chart from JPMorgan’s Kabir Caprihan suggests that bond markets turned on BDCs, compared to other triple-B borrowers, at about the same time that the cost of credit from bank channels jumped:

All of which leaves us scratching our heads about the direction of causation.

Maybe bond and bank lenders are questioning how solid the BDC universe’s middle-market borrowers are in a higher-rate world with greater levels of geopolitical uncertainty, and a business environment [ripe for AI disruption](https://archive.is/o/WOGCt/https://www.ft.com/content/3f8876fb-f951-40cc-a1d6-c4bf0523e795). Sure, individual loan spreads may be tighter, but if the market wanted to price higher levels of default ***correlation***, perhaps wider BDC spreads would be one way of doing this given their [unholy levels of sectoral concentration](https://archive.is/o/WOGCt/https://www.ft.com/content/c60b4ebc-9451-4a30-b8b7-f1e94f851ce2).

Or maybe, as the Fed researchers suggest, banks are using their monopsony power to squeeze the pips of BDC borrowers. And this squeeze has spilled over to bond markets, to the detriment of BDC equity holders.

Given that pretty much the entire BDC business model is to profit from their operators’ supposed superior expertise in organising and negotiating debt financing, it would be quite something if their lackadaisical approach to shopping around for bank financing has led them to pay over the odds on their own cost of borrowing.
