# AI in the life sciences

Standing topic: what generative models can now do in biology, and whether oversight keeps pace. The field's signature tension is that the same capability delivers the therapeutic upside and the misuse risk, and the tools are often released openly.

## Where the capability stands

- **Generative genome design works.** In August 2026 Stanford's **Evo 2** — a genomic language model — designed 16 synthetic bacteriophages that do not exist in nature, and they killed *E. coli* **more effectively than the natural template phage ΦX174**. First demonstration that generative design can produce entire functional genomes, not just proteins ([[2026-08-06 AI creates first synthetic viruses]]).
- Project leader **Brian Hie** says the approach could extend to bacteria and then to more complex organisms, and eventually to designing new organisms without starting from a natural genome ([[2026-08-06 AI creates first synthetic viruses]]).
- Earlier lineage: [[Google DeepMind]]'s AlphaFold won [[Demis Hassabis]] a Nobel Prize in Chemistry for protein-structure prediction ([[2026-08-05 Google DeepMind CEO Demis Hassabis steps aside]]) — structure prediction, where Evo 2 is generation.

## Therapeutic application

**Phage therapy** — using viruses to kill bacteria instead of antibiotics — has been used in eastern Europe for decades but is limited in the west by the need to match specific phages to a patient's bacterial strain and by resistance risk. Generative design could revive it as antibiotic-resistant pathogens spread ([[2026-08-06 AI creates first synthetic viruses]]).

## The governance gap

- Safeguards the Stanford team applied: non-pathogenic *E. coli*, a model **not trained on viruses that infect animal or plant cells**, secure-lab testing ([[2026-08-06 AI creates first synthetic viruses]]).
- But **Evo 2 is open source and free to download**, and Hie argues health benefits outweigh the bioterrorism risk ([[2026-08-06 AI creates first synthetic viruses]]).
- Against that, Thomas Inglesby and Moritz Hanke (Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security) wrote in the same *Science* issue that **"existing governance frameworks are insufficient for overseeing generative genomics"** — the question is no longer whether generative viral genome design exists, but whether oversight can arrive in time ([[2026-08-06 AI creates first synthetic viruses]]).

This is an unresolved contradiction in the record, not a settled position: the researchers' risk assessment and the biosecurity community's are directly opposed, and both are in the same journal issue.

## Watching

- Whether any regulator or funder responds to the Inglesby/Hanke call — and whether DNA-synthesis screening (the practical chokepoint between a designed genome and a physical organism) tightens.
- Extension of generative design to bacteria or eukaryotes, as Hie flagged.
