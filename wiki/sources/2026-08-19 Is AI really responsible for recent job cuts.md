# Is AI really responsible for recent job cuts?

The best sceptical treatment yet of the AI-layoff narrative. Headline tallies: **180,000+ corporate job losses linked to AI since May 2023, 112,000 in 2026 alone** (Challenger, Gray & Christmas); AI was the most-cited layoff reason for the **fifth straight month** in July. Oracle cut 21,000 this year citing AI (opening case: a strategist "pressured to leverage AI… using us to train something that would ultimately replace us"). Salesforce, Lufthansa, Accenture, StanChart also cite AI.

The sceptics' case (strong):
- **Incentives to overclaim**: "AI productivity" sounds better to investors than restructuring or weak performance. Frey (Oxford): "I haven't seen any compelling evidence… I don't think AI-driven automation is the key story." Atlassian's 1,600 cuts to "self-fund further investment in AI"; Meta's January cuts were the metaverse division being defunded.
- **The market doesn't even buy it**: FT analysis — fewer than half of AI-layoff announcements got a next-day share bump, and citing companies **underperformed the Nasdaq by ~10%** over the following 30 sessions.
- **Macro data doesn't show it**: US/UK redundancies not elevated; US initial claims at their lowest weekly level **since 1969** in July; Yale Budget Lab finds no wage/hiring divergence in AI-exposed occupations; Oxford Economics: evidence "patchy," and no productivity acceleration; NBER survey: executives expect **-0.7%** headcount over three years; firms using AI most intensively hired **10% more**.

Where it's real: entry-level hiring in AI-exposed occupations has fallen sharply (Stanford Digital Economy Lab); customer service is being automated by board mandate (Forrester; Salesforce's 4,000 support cuts). The subtler channel: **hiring suppression, not firing** — "companies have got away with not hiring at the usual rate" (Revelio); postings -2.8% US / **-12.6% UK** y/y.

Feeds [[AI and white-collar work]] — significantly complicates the displacement narrative; the "cost of checking didn't fall" theme gets a labour-market sibling: the layoff *announcement* is cheap, the automation is unproven.
